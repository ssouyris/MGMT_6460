Wyndor’s Numbers are Point Estimates

Lecture 01’s plan: \({(x_1, x_2) = (2, 6)}\), $36,000 a week. It uses every hour of Plants 2 and 3.

  • Hours are forecasts. Maintenance and absenteeism move them. Say Plant 2 has \({12 \pm 0.5}\) hours and Plant 3 has \({18 \pm 1}\) (one standard deviation)
  • The window market is a rumor. A competitor may launch a rival window, and Wyndor would have to redesign Product 2 (Windows)

The optimal plan is fragile

  • With normally distributed hours, Plant 2 and Plant 3 each fall short of the plan half the time.
  • \({(2, 6)}\) is feasible in about one week in four: \({0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25}\).

The 0.25 is a Monte Carlo check too: 2 million simulated weeks, 0.2500. An optimal LP solution sits on the boundary by construction, so any binding constraint with a symmetric random right-hand side holds only half the time.

Three Approaches to Address Uncertainty

Attitude The model asks for It needs Section
Plan for the worst case Feasible for every value in a range A range per parameter Robust optimization
Accept a small risk Feasible with probability \({\ge \alpha}\) A distribution per parameter Chance constraints
Decide now, adjust later Best expected profit, with later decisions per scenario Scenarios and probabilities Two-stage recourse

Hard and soft constraints

  • A hard constraint must hold: a contract, a safety limit.
  • A soft constraint can be violated slightly at a cost: overtime, a late order.
  • Robust optimization suits hard constraints; chance constraints and recourse suit soft ones.

Robust Optimization

Interval Uncertainty

Robust optimization

Each uncertain parameter lies in a range (or box) of uncertainty \({[\ell, u]}\) and can take any value in it, independently of the others. Find the best plan that stays feasible for every combination.

  • Suits hard constraints, and parameters with known bounds
  • With \({x \ge 0}\), the worst value of each parameter is one of its two endpoints. Which one depends only on where the parameter sits

The Conservative Counterpart

The conservative counterpart replaces every parameter by its worst value (Soyster, 1973)
Where the parameter sits Worst value Why
\({a_{ij}}\) in a \({\le}\) row largest, \({u}\) uses the most capacity
\({b_i}\) in a \({\le}\) row smallest, \({\ell}\) leaves the least capacity
\({a_{ij}}\), \({b_i}\) in a \({\ge}\) row smallest \({a}\), largest \({b}\) hardest to reach
\({c_j}\) in a max objective smallest, \({\ell}\) guarantees the profit

Equality constraints

An equation cannot hold for every value in a range. The method needs every constraint in \({\le}\) or \({\ge}\) form.

The price of robustness

It guards against all nine parameters at their worst in the same week: a rare week, and the protection can cost more than it is worth.

Soyster: Every Coefficient at Its Worst

Bertsimas and Sim (2004) model each uncertain coefficient of row \({i}\) as \({\tilde a_{ij} = \bar a_{ij} + \eta_{ij}\,\hat a_{ij}}\): estimate \({\bar a_{ij}}\), largest deviation \({\hat a_{ij}}\), unknown \({\eta_{ij} \in [-1, 1]}\).

  • With \({x \ge 0}\) the worst case is \({\eta_{ij} = 1}\) for every uncertain coefficient: \({\;\sum_j \bar a_{ij} x_j + \sum_{j \in J_i} \hat a_{ij} x_j \le b_i}\) (Soyster, 1973)
  • Plant 3, both \({\pm 0.5}\): \({\;3x_1 + 2x_2 + 0.5x_1 + 0.5x_2 \le 18}\); at \({(2, 6)}\) the left side is 22

The objection

All coefficients at their worst in the same week. With 100 of them in a row, that week never comes.

A Budget of \({\Gamma}\) Deviations

Let nature move at most \({\Gamma_i}\) coefficients of row \({i}\) to their worst values; a fractional \({\Gamma_i}\) moves one more part of the way.

\[ \sum_j \bar a_{ij} x_j + \beta_i(x, \Gamma_i) \le b_i, \qquad \beta_i(x, \Gamma_i) = \max_{S \subseteq J_i,\ |S| \le \Gamma_i} \; \sum_{j \in S} \hat a_{ij}\, x_j \]

\({\Gamma}\) \({\beta}\) at \({(2, 6)}\) Plant 3 row
0 0 18 fits
0.5 1.5 19.5 fails
1 3 21 fails
2 3 + 1 = 4 22 fails
  • Deviations at \({(2, 6)}\): \({\;0.5 \cdot 2 = 1}\) and \({0.5 \cdot 6 = 3}\)
  • Nature spends its budget on the largest deviation first
  • \({\Gamma = 0}\): the nominal row; \({\Gamma = |J_i|}\): Soyster

The Protection Is an LP

For a fixed plan \({x}\), the worst case is a small LP (Proposition 1). Let \({u_j \in [0, 1]}\) be the share of its range that coefficient \({j}\) moves:

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_i(x, \Gamma_i) \;=\; \max_{u} \;& \sum_{j \in J_i} \hat a_{ij} x_j\, u_j \\ \text{s.t.} \;& \sum_{j \in J_i} u_j \le \Gamma_i \\ & 0 \le u_j \le 1, \quad j \in J_i \end{aligned} \]

  • Solve it greedily: \({u_j = 1}\) on the largest deviations until the budget runs out, a fraction on the next one
  • At \({(2, 6)}\) with \({\Gamma = 1.5}\): \({\;u_2 = 1}\), \({u_1 = 0.5}\), so \({\beta = 3 + 0.5 = 3.5}\)

Still not an LP in x

A \({\max}\) inside a constraint, with products \({x_j u_j}\): not an LP in \({x}\).

Duality Removes the Max (Theorem 1)

The dual of the protection LP (lecture 04) has the same optimal value:

\[ \beta_i(x, \Gamma_i) \;=\; \min_{z_i,\, p_{ij} \ge 0} \; \Gamma_i z_i + \sum_{j \in J_i} p_{ij} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad z_i + p_{ij} \ge \hat a_{ij} x_j \]

A \({\min}\) on the left of \({\le b_i}\) holds if some \({z_i, p_{ij}}\) fit, so the robust model is one LP:

\[ \begin{alignat*}{3} \max~ & \rlap{c^\top x} \\[4pt] \text{s.t.}~ & \textstyle\sum_j \bar a_{ij} x_j + \Gamma_i z_i + \sum_{j \in J_i} p_{ij} & \quad & {}\le b_i && \qquad \text{every row } i \\ & z_i + p_{ij} & \quad & {}\ge \hat a_{ij} x_j && \qquad \text{every row } i,\ j \in J_i \\ & x, z, p & \quad & {}\ge 0 \end{alignat*} \]

  • Plant 3, \({\Gamma = 1}\): \({(1, 6)}\), \({Z = 33}\); \({\;z = 0.5}\), \({p = (0, 2.5)}\): \({\;15 + 0.5 + 2.5 = 18}\)

One z_i per row and one p_ij per uncertain coefficient: an LP stays an LP, a MIP stays a MIP. The dual optimum is not unique: at (1, 6) with Gamma = 1, z = 3, p = (0, 0) also gives 3.

How Large Should \({\Gamma}\) Be?

For independent, symmetric deviations, \({P(\text{row } i \text{ fails}) \le e^{-\Gamma_i^2 / (2|J_i|)}}\) for any plan.

\({\lvert J_i \rvert}\) \({\Gamma_i}\) for \({\le 1\%}\) Share
10 8.2 82%
100 24.3 24%
200 33.9 17%
2,000 105 5%
  • Table 1 of the paper, Theorem 3 bound
  • Knapsack, 200 items, weights \({\pm 10\%}\): Soyster costs 5.5%; a 0.57% violation bound costs 1.54%

The price of robustness

For a fixed risk, \({\Gamma_i}\) grows like \({\sqrt{|J_i|}}\): rows with many uncertain coefficients get protection cheaply.

Chance Constraints

When a Range Has No End

  • Robust optimization needs a smallest and a largest value
  • Hours that are normally distributed have neither: the worst case is \({-\infty}\)
  • Accept a small probability of a shortfall instead

Chance constraint (Charnes and Cooper, 1959)

For a confidence level \({\alpha}\), such as 0.95, require \[ P\Big(\sum_j a_{ij} x_j \le b_i\Big) \;\ge\; \alpha . \]

The Deterministic Equivalent

Let \({b_i \sim \N(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)}\) and \({L = \sum_j a_{ij} x_j}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P(b_i \ge L) \ge \alpha &\iff \frac{L - \mu_i}{\sigma_i} \le -z_\alpha \\ &\iff \sum_j a_{ij} x_j \;\le\; \mu_i - z_\alpha \sigma_i \end{aligned} \]

  • A linear constraint with a smaller right-hand side
  • For a \({\ge}\) row: \({\;\sum_j a_{ij} x_j \ge \mu_i + z_\alpha \sigma_i}\)

Standard normal table

\({z_{0.90} = 1.282, \quad z_{0.95} = 1.645, \quad z_{0.975} = 1.960, \quad z_{0.99} = 2.326}\)

Joint chance constraint

All rows with probability 0.95: each binding row needs \({\sqrt{0.95} = 0.9747}\), so \({z = 1.955}\) and \({Z = 32.580}\).

Each row’s chance constraint is separate; the joint probability is a product only because the hours are independent and only two rows bind. With correlated hours, or uncertain coefficients a_ij, the deterministic equivalent is no longer linear: a normal a_ij gives a square-root term in x, a second-order cone constraint.

Two-Stage Recourse

Wyndor’s Rumor

A competitor may launch a window that competes with Product 2. If it does, Wyndor redesigns the window: Plant 3 needs 6 hours per batch and the profit falls to $1,000.

Doors are unaffected
Scenario Probability Plant 3 hours per window batch Profit per window batch
1: no competitor 1/4 2 5
2: competitor 3/4 6 1
  • Doors must start now; window production can wait until the rumor resolves

Each Scenario Alone

Scenario 1, no competitor

\[ \begin{aligned} \max~ Z = 3x_1 &+ 5x_2 \\ x_1 &\le 4 \\ 2x_2 &\le 12 \\ 3x_1 + 2x_2 &\le 18 \end{aligned} \] \({(2, 6)}\), \({Z = 36}\)

Scenario 2, competitor

\[ \begin{aligned} \max~ Z = 3x_1 &+ 1x_2 \\ x_1 &\le 4 \\ 2x_2 &\le 12 \\ 3x_1 + 6x_2 &\le 18 \end{aligned} \] \({(4, 1)}\), \({Z = 13}\)

Two answers for one decision

Doors start before the rumor resolves, so \({x_1}\) must be one number. The scenarios disagree: 2 batches or 4?

Here-and-Now and Wait-and-See

Two-stage stochastic program

  • First stage, here-and-now: \({x_1}\), one value, before the scenario is known
  • Second stage, the recourse: \({x_{2s}}\), one value per scenario, after it is known
  • Maximize the first-stage profit plus the expected second-stage profit (Dantzig, 1955)

The Extensive Form

\[ \begin{aligned} \max~ Z = 3x_1 &+ \tfrac14\,(5x_{21}) + \tfrac34\,(1x_{22}) \\ \text{s.t.} \quad x_1 &\le 4 \\ 2x_{21} &\le 12 && \text{scenario 1} \\ 3x_1 + 2x_{21} &\le 18 && \text{scenario 1} \\ 2x_{22} &\le 12 && \text{scenario 2} \\ 3x_1 + 6x_{22} &\le 18 && \text{scenario 2} \\ x_1,\; x_{21},\; x_{22} &\ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

  • One LP: a copy of each scenario’s constraints, all sharing \({x_1}\)
  • Each scenario’s profit is weighted by its probability

Solution

\({x_1 = 4}\); \({\;x_{21} = 3}\) if no competitor; \({\;x_{22} = 1}\) if competitor. Expected profit 16.5: $16,500 a week.

Solving It by Hand

Fix \({x_1}\); each scenario fills its tightest plant:

\[ x_{21} = \min\big(6, \tfrac{18 - 3x_1}{2}\big), \qquad x_{22} = \min\big(6, \tfrac{18 - 3x_1}{6}\big) \]

\({x_1}\) No comp. Comp. Expected

On \({[2, 4]}\) the expected profit is \({13.5 + 0.75x_1}\): it rises, so \({x_1 = 4}\).

On [2, 4]: 3x1 + (1/4)(5)(18 − 3x1)/2 + (3/4)(1)(18 − 3x1)/6 = 3x1 + (3/4)(18 − 3x1) = 13.5 + 0.75 x1. On [0, 2] it is 9.75 + 2.625 x1. Both pieces rise, so the maximum is at x1 = 4.

  3. If all three rows bind, with what probability do they hold together?

The Value of Information

Four Benchmark Values

Name Definition Farmer ($)
EV Solve at the mean yields 118,600
EEV Plant the EV acres, adapt in each scenario 107,240
RP Solve the extensive form 108,390
WS Average of each scenario’s own optimum 115,406

Birge and Louveaux, ch. 4

For a max, \({\text{EEV} \le \text{RP} \le \text{WS}}\). Value of the stochastic solution \({\text{VSS} = \text{RP} - \text{EEV}}\); expected value of perfect information \({\text{EVPI} = \text{WS} - \text{RP}}\).

VSS and EVPI on One Axis

  • VSS $1,150: what modeling the yields as uncertain earns over planting for the mean
  • EVPI $7,016: the most a perfect weather forecast is worth per season
  • EV $118,600 is a promise no season keeps

Reading the Two Numbers

Model VSS EVPI Reading

Farmer 1,150 7,016 The mean plan loses money; a forecast would be worth more

  • Large EVPI: information pays. A forecast, a test market, a delay
  • Both small: plan for the mean and move on

The Newsvendor

The Smallest Recourse Model

A store orders \({q}\) units before demand \({D}\) is known; it sells \({\min(q, D)}\) and salvages the rest.

  • Price 48, cost 22, salvage 8 ($ per unit)
  • Underage cost \({c_u = 48 - 22 = 26}\): a sale lost
  • Overage cost \({c_o = 22 - 8 = 14}\): a unit left over
  • One more unit pays if \({\;c_u\,P(D > q) \ge c_o\,P(D \le q)}\)

Critical fractile

\[ P(D \le q^*) = \frac{c_u}{c_u + c_o} = \frac{26}{40} = 0.65 \] With \({D \sim \N(500, 100^2)}\): \({q^* = 500 + 0.385 \times 100 = 538.5}\).

First stage q; second stage the sales, one per demand outcome: the farmer’s structure with one product and a continuum of scenarios.

From prediction to decision

A forecasting or machine learning model that samples possible futures feeds a stochastic program directly: each sample is a scenario. The model predicts; the optimization decides.

Sample Average Approximation

SAA

Draw \({N}\) scenarios, give each probability \({1/N}\), and solve the extensive form (Kleywegt et al., 2002).

  • Each dot: one sample of \({N}\) demands
  • \({q_N \to q^*}\) as \({N}\) grows
  • Judge a plan on fresh samples

Conclusions

Summary of Part I

Core takeaways

  • Robust: each parameter at its worst value
  • \({\Gamma}\) caps how many deviate at once
  • Chance: right-hand side \({\mu_i - z_\alpha \sigma_i}\)
  • Recourse: first stage once, second stage per scenario

Watch out for

  • The worst value depends on \({\le}\) or \({\ge}\)
  • Separate rows at \({\alpha}\) hold jointly with less
  • First-stage variables: no scenario index
  • Constraints hold in every scenario

Summary of Part II

Core takeaways

  • VSS = RP − EEV; EVPI = WS − RP
  • \({P(D \le q^*) = c_u / (c_u + c_o)}\)
  • SAA: judge on fresh samples

Watch out for

  • EV is not achievable
  • Index the second stage by scenario
  • Check AI answers with a solver

Bottom line

Decide what kind of uncertainty you face before you choose a model: ranges, distributions or scenarios.
